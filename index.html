Flagship Policy

A Community Grocery Store for Ward 7

Food prices are out of control. A single person on Ontario Works receives $832 a month, but an average London rent is $1,032 and a basic monthly food basket costs $418. The math doesn't work. We can change it.

I'm campaigning to bring a publicly-anchored community grocery store to Ward 7 — a model that uses city land and public support to eliminate overhead costs and pass the savings directly to residents buying food. This isn't a charity or a food bank. It's a neighbourhood grocery store that puts community before profit.

In New York City, Mayor Zohran Mamdani has already begun implementing this exact model — city-owned land, private operator, no rent, no property taxes, groceries at affordable prices. London can lead in Ontario the same way New York is leading in the United States. Ward 7 can be where it starts here.

How the Model Works

01

City or Municipally-Owned Land

The store operates on city-held or partnered land in Ward 7, eliminating the rent and property tax burden that drives up grocery prices.

02

Community Operator with Price Standards

A co-operative, non-profit, or community operator runs the store under a mandate to sell staples at wholesale or near-cost prices.

03

Revenue & Cost-Recovery for the City

A lease arrangement or economic development structure allows the city to recover costs over time — reducing the net burden on taxpayers while delivering real affordability.

04

Jobs & Local Sourcing

Prioritize local suppliers, living wages for workers, and integration with the London Food Coalition's existing rescue and distribution network.

Precedents That Work

NYC (2026): Mayor Mamdani has allocated $70 million for five city-owned stores, eliminating rent and property taxes to lower grocery prices across boroughs.

Co-op Models: Worker and consumer co-operatives across Canada have demonstrated that community-controlled grocery retail is operationally viable and builds local wealth.

The London Case: With the city already spending millions supporting housing through LMCH, food retail is the logical next frontier for public intervention in affordability.

What the Budget Shows

The Choices Being Made — and the Ones We Should Be Making

The 2026 budget isn't just numbers — it's priorities. Here's what's actually in it and what I would push to change.

Problem

Recreation Fees Up 7% on Average

City Council directed a 7% average fee increase on recreation and sport services to generate $1.5 million in revenue. That means families in Ward 7 pay more to access community centres, arenas, and pools — exactly the spaces that are supposed to bring people together regardless of income.

Budget Case #P-19 — $1.52M savings to city, at residents' expense

My Position

Protect Recreation Access as a Public Good

Recreation programs should not be priced out of reach for working families. I will advocate for income-scaled fees — where higher-income users subsidize access for lower-income residents — rather than across-the-board hikes that disproportionately hurt Ward 7 households.

Problem

Climate Change Reserve Fund Eliminated

The city has eliminated its $192,000 annual contribution to the Climate Change Reserve Fund as a budget-cutting measure. This fund paid for emissions reduction projects and helped unlock grant funding. Ward 7 residents will live with the consequences of delayed climate action.

Budget Case #P-24 — saves $192K/yr, risks millions in grant leverage

My Position

Restore Climate Funding, Seek Provincial Grants

Eliminating the Climate Change Reserve is short-sighted. This fund was used to leverage grants and implement the Climate Emergency Action Plan. I would push to restore the contribution and tie it to active grant applications that return more than we invest.

Problem

Police Budget Growing Faster Than Housing

London Police Services' 2026 budget sits at $195.7 million — growing at 4.9% — while London & Middlesex Community Housing (LMCH) received only $1.4 million in additional support to prevent service cuts. The city's priorities are visible in these numbers.

LPS: $195.7M | LMCH additional support: $1.4M

My Position

Rebalance Toward Housing & Prevention

Stable housing prevents the downstream crises that drive up policing and emergency services costs. I will advocate at Council for proportional investment in LMCH and affordable housing, recognizing it as a more cost-effective intervention than reactive policing.

Problem

Transit Cuts While Ridership Matters

A $700,000 one-time London Transit Commission budget reduction has been approved. For residents in Ward 7 who rely on transit to reach groceries, jobs, and services, any reduction in service reliability hits hardest.

Budget Case #P-18 — $700K cut to LTC operations

My Position

Transit Investment Is Affordability Policy

A car-free or car-limited resident cannot afford service cuts. I will oppose transit reductions and advocate for LTC investment as part of an integrated affordability agenda — because getting to a grocery store or a job without a car is not a luxury, it's a necessity.

02

Property Tax Fairness & Revenue Alternatives

With property taxes already projected to grow 3.4% in 2026 and average 6% annually through 2027, the burden on Ward 7 homeowners and renters is real. I will push the city to develop non-tax revenue streams — including community enterprise models like the grocery store — and advocate for development charges that ensure growth pays for growth, rather than loading costs onto existing residents. Long-term, the community grocery generates city revenue that can help hold down property tax increases.

Fiscal
03

Affordable Housing & LMCH Support

London and Middlesex Community Housing is under serious financial pressure — rising property taxes on the homes it owns are eating into its ability to maintain services. I will advocate for full and sustainable LMCH funding at Council, and push for more ambitious affordable housing targets as part of the capital plan. The 2026 budget puts only $1.4 million toward stabilizing LMCH. That is nowhere near enough given the scale of London's housing crisis.

Housing
04

Transit Access & Active Transportation

Public transit is infrastructure for people who don't own cars — disproportionately lower-income residents, seniors, students, and newcomers. Opposing transit cuts and advocating for route frequency and reliability in Ward 7 is part of a coherent affordability agenda. So is safe cycling infrastructure and pedestrian investment, which reduces the cost of living for people who don't want or can't afford to own a vehicle.

Mobility
05

Climate Action & Neighbourhood Resilience

Eliminating the Climate Change Reserve Fund contribution is a false economy. Climate inaction costs more than climate action — in emergency repairs, health impacts, and lost grant opportunities. I will push to restore climate funding, implement the Climate Emergency Action Plan in Ward 7, and advocate for green retrofits of social housing that lower energy costs for LMCH tenants. The communities that suffer most from climate disasters are the same communities that suffer most from affordability crises — that connection must shape our budget decisions.
